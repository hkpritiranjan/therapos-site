Product Review: Six Lenses

A six-lens review of TherapOS from the founder, product manager, visual designer, UX designer, therapist and patient perspectives, with ranked actions and a presentation guide.

ℹ
Scope and method

Reviewed 25 October 2026 through six lenses. Evidence: the live docs site, the app repository (code read; the app was not run end-to-end), one reproduced bug, WCAG contrast calculations, and the Research page. Scores are my judgment, not measurements. Nothing here is validated with real therapists or patients yet, and that is the biggest finding of all.

Scorecard

LensScoreOne-line verdict
Founder3 / 5Sharp wedge and unusually good documentation, but zero customer evidence and thin unit economics at $29.
Product manager3 / 5Right problem, wrong sequencing: growth features are planned before trust and access gates. Plan cap and trial length conflict with real usage.
Visual / product designer2 → 4 / 5The old dark dev-tool look and failing contrast suited neither the audience nor the topic. Redesigned this session, including the app UI (light theme, new tokens, AA contrast).
UX designer2.5 / 5Good core flow (draft → edit → confirm → finalize), but missing login, settings, addenda, AI-transparency and accessibility basics. The draft-parsing bug that broke the headline demo is now fixed.

Patient (consumer)2 / 5Never sees the product but bears its risk. Consent, accuracy of what is written about them, and opt-out are all missing.
✓
Fixed (25 Oct 2026): drafts landed as raw JSON

The API prompts the model to return JSON for SOAP, DAP and BIRP notes, but the session page's parser expected labelled prose such as "Subjective: …", so the entire raw JSON string was placed in the first field. The live model also wraps its answer in a ```json fence. Now fixed in apps/web/lib/draft-parser.ts: it parses complete JSON first (including fenced output), tolerates JSON that is still streaming so the live preview reads as clean text, and falls back to the old labelled-prose parser. Seven regression tests pass (npm test -w @therapos/web), and I verified it against real streamed output from the live model.

1. Founder lens

What is strong

  • A real wedge. Text-first, no recording, EHR-agnostic. It is a clear answer to the question every therapist asks first: "does it record my sessions?"
  • Speed. A working draft-to-memory loop in four days, with honest documentation of what it does and does not do.
  • Discipline about scope. The "will not build" list is a genuine strategic asset.

What worries me

  • Zero customer evidence. The plan was written before any therapist interview. Every key number is an assumption.
  • Low revenue ceiling. 1,500 to 4,000 paying seats yields only $0.4M to $1.4M a year (Research §2). Customer acquisition has to stay near zero-cost, so community and referral channels are not optional.
  • Compliance before revenue. Counsel, BAAs, audit logging, and a security assessment cost real money and months, before the first paid seat.
  • Price anchor risk. EHR-bundled AI is $35 to $40 on top of an EHR the therapist already pays for. If TherapOS is "just notes" it is competing with something that feels free.

Pre-mortem: the five most likely reasons this fails

  1. Therapists do not trust an unknown startup with client information, so activation stalls at "I tried it with fake data."
  2. Typing raw notes saves too little time compared with ambient scribes, so there is no strong reason to switch.
  3. Compliance work consumes the runway before product-market fit.
  4. An EHR vendor ships "good enough" memory inside the product therapists already use.
  5. Low ARPU plus paid acquisition never pays back.

TestContinue ifRethink if

Thresholds are proposals, not industry standards, except the 40% "very disappointed" benchmark, which is widely used as a rough signal. Agree them before the tests start.

Unit economics to measure, not assume

AI cost per finalized note = (draft tokens + prep tokens + summary tokens + embedding tokens) × provider price, plus retries. Track it from the first beta session. Gross margin at $29 should be comfortable at 100 notes a month, but only a measurement will tell, and the figure depends on provider choice and prompt length.

2. Product manager lens

Job to be done

After a session

"When I finish a client, I want a compliant progress note done in a few minutes, in my own voice, so I can leave on time and not carry the work home."

Before a session

"When a client is about to arrive, I want to remember where we left off, what I assigned, and what to check in on, without rereading five notes."

The two jobs form a loop: write after → prepared before. The prep brief is the retention hook, because it only has value if notes were finalized the week before. Design the onboarding to reach "second session for the same client" as fast as possible.

Metrics

  • North Star: finalized notes per active therapist per week.
  • Activation: a finalized note within the first session of use, and a prep brief opened before a repeat client.
  • Quality guardrails: share of draft words kept, time to finalize, and reports of inaccuracies.
  • Counter-metric: notes finalized with zero edits (a possible rubber-stamping signal).

Packaging problems found in our own plan

⚠
Decided: the Solo cap contradicted our own persona

The plan's core persona sees 25 clients a week, about 107 sessions a month. The Solo plan caps AI notes at 100 a month. The target customer would hit the cap in the third week. Options: a fair-use "unlimited" (for example a soft limit near 200), a 150 cap, or pricing by seat with no cap. Decision applied (25 Oct 2026): Solo now includes up to 200 AI notes a month. Clinic stays unlimited.

⚠
Decided: a 14-day trial was short for a weekly-cadence product

The memory and prep value appears at a client's second or third session. With weekly sessions, 14 days gives most therapists one repeat visit, if that. Decision applied: the trial is now 30 days. "First 10 finalized notes free" remains a good alternative to test, since it measures value instead of time.

3. Visual and product designer lens

Diagnosis

  • Wrong emotional register. Near-black slate with indigo reads as a developer tool. A mental-health product has to feel calm, warm, and safe, for the therapist and, indirectly, the client.
  • Accessibility failure. The previous muted text colour measured 3.06 : 1 on the page background and 2.84 : 1 on cards. WCAG AA requires 4.5 : 1 for body text. The new palette's lowest body-text ratio is 5.03 : 1.
  • Emoji as icons. Inconsistent across platforms and, in testing, rendered as empty boxes. Replaced with one consistent line-icon set.
  • Generic template feel. No illustration, no brand shapes, no warmth.

What changed

The visual system now draws on two wellness-app references you supplied: deep navy, periwinkle, sunshine yellow and teal from the first, plus blush pink, peach and lavender from the second. Alegreya (a humanist serif) is paired with Poppins for a warm, editorial feel. Cards are rounded, colour is used to organise content rather than decorate it, and flower, blob and cloud shapes give the brand a human, hand-made quality.

Design principle: two moods, one system

The reference apps are patient-facing and playful. A clinician's tool needs to be calmer and denser: long reading, careful editing, daytime offices. So: the marketing site and docs use the full expressive palette; the product UI should use the same tokens with lower saturation, more whitespace, and a light theme by default. The app was dark-only (className="dark", dark tokens in globals.css) and failed the contrast check above. It now uses the same palette as a calmer light theme: cream surfaces, plum text, periwinkle actions, rounded cards, Poppins with Alegreya headings, and AA-safe status colours.

Next design steps

  • Done: new tokens and light theme in apps/web/app/globals.css. Still to do: an optional dark theme, and a review of every inline-styled component.
  • Commission or adapt a small illustration set (calm figures, plants, clouds). Do not use stock "therapy couch" imagery.
  • Define a type scale and spacing scale in one place, and document components in the design-system page.
  • Check all status colours for colour-blind safety by adding an icon or label to every badge.

The flow, redesigned

  1. Before

    Open the client, see a three-line prep brief with links to the notes it came from, plus last session's homework status.

  2. During or right after

    Type or dictate rough notes. A small panel shows exactly which fields will be sent to the AI, with a per-client "AI off" switch.

  3. Draft

    The draft streams into the right structure. Placeholder gaps are highlighted. Stop, regenerate, and tone options are one click away.

  4. Review and sign

    A diff view shows what the therapist changed. The note is signed with a visible AI-assisted marker. Later changes are addenda.

  5. Hand off

    "Copy for SimplePractice" (or TherapyNotes, or plain text) puts correctly formatted sections on the clipboard.

A note on psychiatrists

The plan is built for psychotherapists, and the roadmap explicitly excludes medication management. A psychiatrist's workflow is different: short medication-management visits, mental status exams, side-effect tracking, labs, e-prescribing and diagnosis coding. TherapOS today has none of this, and its "no diagnosis" rule conflicts with how psychiatric notes work. If psychiatrists are a target, treat them as a separate ICP that needs note templates (MSE, medication review), different prompts, and a regulatory review. Otherwise, say so out loud: "therapists first, psychiatry later or never."

Patients never log in, but every word the product writes is about them.

Should there be a patient-facing app?

The two reference designs you shared are patient-facing: mood check-ins, guided sessions, playlists. That is a tempting direction (homework adherence, between-session engagement), but it would contradict the plan's "no patient portal" line, double the support surface, change the buyer, and bring chatbot and wellness regulation into scope. Recommendation: use that look and warmth for the brand, keep the product therapist-first, and revisit a patient companion only after the clinician loop is proven. If you do build it, the first feature should be homework and mood check-ins assigned by the therapist, not a free-standing wellness app.

8. Presenting this

Story arc (about 10 minutes)

  1. The human problem. Therapists finish the day and then start writing. Show the 6 p.m. scene.
  2. The landscape. AI adoption is rising fast, but nine in ten clinicians are worried. Trust is the gap.
  3. What we found. Competitors already do cross-session memory, so we do not claim a feature gap. We claim a trust position: no recording, any EHR, inspectable memory.
  4. What we built. Walk the five-step loop. Show the design and the case study.
  5. What is honest. Not yet compliant, not yet validated, one demo-blocking bug being fixed. Showing the status table builds credibility.
  6. The plan. The 60-day validation plan with kill criteria, then the three gates.
  7. The ask. Name it: introductions to 15 therapists, a legal review, or a decision on provider and pricing.

Hard questions, honest answers

QuestionAnswer
"Isn't this just Upheal or Mentalyc?"They record sessions and are strong on cross-session tracking. We do not record anything and work beside any EHR. Whether that is enough is exactly what the 60-day test measures.
"Is it HIPAA compliant?"No, not yet, and we say so. It is a launch gate: audit logs, MFA, encryption controls and executed BAAs before any real patient data.
"What if the AI is wrong?"It is always a draft. The therapist edits and signs. Gaps are marked "[Therapist to complete]". We are adding provenance links and an AI-assisted marker.
"How big is the market?"Roughly 150,000 to 400,000 independent US therapists by our model. At 1% penetration and $29 a month that is $0.4M to $1.4M a year, so clinic accounts matter.
"Why not use audio, like everyone else?"Consent, retention and the psychotherapy-notes question. Text-first removes them. Dictation will close the time gap.
"What have you actually built?"A working draft-to-memory loop, ~5,700 lines, 9 commits in four days, plus full documentation. Login, billing, search and compliance controls are not built. See the Case Study status table.
"What about patients?"They never see it, but they are affected. We plan a consent template, per-client opt-out, and pseudonymised AI calls.

Live demo checklist

  • Start Postgres and Redis (docker compose up), apply migrations, run the API (port 3001) and web app (port 3000), and set OPENAI_API_KEY.
  • Use fictional data only. No real client information in a demo or a beta without BAAs.
  • The JSON parsing bug is fixed. Rehearse once end-to-end anyway; have screenshots as a fallback if the network or the AI service fails.
